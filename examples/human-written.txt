The Wednesday Table

My grandmother could not read the bus timetable. She had been in this country for eleven years by then and she still could not read it, and every Wednesday she took the 14A to the community centre anyway, counting stops under her breath.

I was nine when she started bringing me along. My job, as she explained it, was to hold the transfer ticket. This was not really my job. My job was to read the signs.

We never discussed this arrangement. She would look at a sign, then look at me, and I would say the name of the street, and she would nod as though confirming something she already knew. For three years I believed I was helping her. It took me until high school to understand that she had memorised the route within a month, and that what she actually needed was for me to keep talking.

At the community centre she taught a knitting class to six other women. I sat in the corner with my homework and listened to them argue in four languages about whether a particular stitch was worth the trouble. Mrs. Adeyemi thought it was not. My grandmother thought it was. Nobody ever won, which I now think was the point.

The knitting was not the point either. One Wednesday a woman I had never seen came in and sat down and did not pick up any needles. She just sat. My grandmother poured her tea and kept teaching, and did not ask her anything at all, and the woman came back the next week, and the week after that, and eventually she picked up the needles.

I asked my grandmother once why she never asked that woman what was wrong. She said, in Punjabi, that a question is a door you hold open, and if you push someone through it you have not really opened anything.

I have thought about that sentence more than any lesson I received in school.

When I began tutoring at the refugee resettlement centre last year, I arrived with a curriculum. I had planned units. I had assessment rubrics I was proud of. In my second week a fifteen-year-old named Behzad told me, very politely, that he did not want to learn the past perfect tense, and I discovered that my rubrics had no row for that.

So I put them away and asked him what he did want. He wanted to know how to argue with his landlord. We spent four sessions on that. His English did not improve measurably. He got his radiator fixed.

I am still not sure whether that was good teaching. I am fairly sure it was the right thing to do, and I have started to suspect those are different questions, and that most of the interesting problems live in the gap between them.

My grandmother died in March. I took the 14A to her service, and I counted the stops.
